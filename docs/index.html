I study how financial conditions and public policy shape capital allocation, household decisions, and firm behavior. My research connects open-economy macroeconomics, macro-finance, and industrial organization, with a particular interest in China’s financial system—its banking structure, credit allocation, and policy transmission. Current projects examine China’s housing adjustment, the design of industrial support, and how AI changes learning and competition.

I received my Ph.D. in World Economics from Renmin University of China in 2024. I am currently a Post-doctoral Fellow at the Institute of China Economy, The University of Hong Kong.

Research interests: Open-economy macroeconomics; macro-finance; China’s financial system; industrial organization; AI and the economy.

Publications

Age structure and economic growth: The quantification of China’s demographic dividends

Zijun Wang, Qingsong Yao, and Guoqing Zhao

Economic and Political Studies, 2024, 12(4): 463–481.

How much of China’s economic growth can be attributed to changes in its population’s age composition? This paper develops a model linking demographic transitions to household consumption, saving, and growth, and uses national data for 1991–2019 to quantify the demographic contribution. It examines how changes in labor supply and saving jointly supported growth, and documents the subsequent transition from a demographic dividend to an ageing-related drag. The analysis connects population structure to the sources of aggregate growth.

Journal article

Population ageing and household risk aversion

Zijun Wang, Qingsong Yao, and Guoqing Zhao

Applied Economics Letters, 2025, 32(15): 2206–2215.

Population ageing can change financial decisions through the composition of the household, as well as through an individual’s own age. Using Chinese household survey data, this paper estimates the relationship between the share of family members aged 65 and above and the probability of reporting risk-averse preferences. A larger elderly share is associated with greater risk aversion and a preference for safer investments. The analysis also considers how the strength of this relationship varies with the broader demographic transition, linking household composition to portfolio choice.

Working Papers

Asset Bubbles, International Capital Flow and Business Cycle

Ph.D. dissertation and working manuscript

Why can expanding asset markets coexist with capital flowing out of an economy? This project studies how asset bubbles change domestic financing conditions and the allocation of capital across countries. It develops a two-country macroeconomic framework with financial frictions, linking the liquidity services of bubble assets to investment, borrowing, and cross-border financing. The dissertation combines these mechanisms with empirical analysis using international and China–US data. The working manuscript focuses on how changes in domestic asset valuations can alter financing demand and transmit business-cycle fluctuations across economies.

Housing Market Liquidity, Labor Misallocation, and TFP

With Zhiyong Fan

Housing supports production and borrowing, but a more valuable housing sector can also change where people choose to work. This project brings housing’s collateral services into a growth framework with labor allocation across housing-related activity, manufacturing, and research. It examines how the financing benefits of housing interact with wages, occupational choices, and the cost of research labor. The central question is when an expansion that eases financing also draws resources away from innovation, and how the balance between these channels determines the long-run levels of output and technology.

Working manuscript under revision

Financial Asymmetry and Policy Spillovers: A DSGE Model of Strong Central Bank

Why does monetary policy in a financially dominant economy constrain policy and amplify fluctuations elsewhere? This project develops a two-country model in which banks face different financing frictions and unequal access to cross-border funding. It follows the transmission from foreign borrowing costs and exchange-rate movements to bank balance sheets, domestic credit, and investment. The analysis compares how financial frictions and restrictions on international funding change the receiving economy’s exposure, asking which domestic financial conditions strengthen or weaken monetary spillovers.

AI & the Economy

AI, Learning Capacity, and the Absorption of Knowledge

Theoretical work in progress · Macroeconomics and human capital

AI may change not only what people produce today, but also how effectively they acquire knowledge tomorrow. This project distinguishes knowledge already retained from the capacity to absorb further knowledge through active interaction with AI. A heterogeneous human-capital framework studies how these two processes shape aggregate productivity and convergence toward an expanding knowledge frontier. It considers differences in learning responsiveness and the possibility that greater reliance on AI reduces active engagement. The central question is when immediate assistance becomes a lasting improvement in productive capability, and when similar current productivity gains conceal different paths of knowledge accumulation.

Open Weights, Competition, and Innovation in AI

Research agenda · Industrial organization

Why do AI developers release model weights while retaining commercial products and services? This project examines how openness changes the returns firms can capture from innovation and how those incentives interact with competition. It distinguishes access to model weights from unrestricted commercial use, and separates frontier-model development from adaptation, hosting, and downstream applications. Initial data construction brings together model capabilities, weight availability, developer identities, and listed API prices. The aim is to understand when openness encourages complementary development and adoption, and when imitation or weaker revenue capture may constrain subsequent investment in model improvement.

China’s Economy

China’s Housing Adjustment: Who Gained, Who Bears the Losses?

Work in progress · Housing policy and the macroeconomy

How are the gains from a housing boom distributed, and who bears the losses when the arrangements supporting that boom change? This project investigates whether land, credit, and public-service arrangements become capitalized into housing prices while creating different opportunities for households and firms to benefit. It distinguishes gains realized through sales or collateral-financed business activity from the obligations of later buyers who acquired housing at higher prices. The proposed framework follows these balance sheets through a housing adjustment, asking how declining asset values, outstanding debt, and financing conditions transmit losses to consumption, investment, and the wider economy.

Industrial Policy, Product Choice, and Excess Capacity in China

Work in progress · Industrial organization and industrial policy

Industrial policy can rapidly mobilize investment and build an industry, yet the resulting capacity may serve closely overlapping markets. This project asks how support for entry and expansion affects firms’ choices between replicating existing products, improving quality, and developing new demand. It links these choices to physical investment, price competition, capacity utilization, and subsequent adjustment. The proposed empirical approach connects products, firms, and plants to distinguish business stealing from market expansion. Policy comparisons would examine whether changing support for capacity, innovation, and adjustment improves welfare, allowing for the possibility that standardization and intense competition themselves generate valuable cost reductions and learning.

Macro-Finance

Debt-Service Burdens During a Housing Adjustment

Work in progress · Mortgage contracts and bank transmission

Falling house prices and household income do not automatically change payments on outstanding mortgages. This project studies how existing debt contracts transmit a housing adjustment to consumption and bank credit. It distinguishes scheduled principal repayment, voluntary prepayment, new lending, and the repricing of old loans. The central comparison considers policies that provide similar immediate relief to borrowers but affect future repayments and banks’ retained earnings differently. By recording both sides of each payment, the framework investigates when household cash-flow relief and bank lending responses reinforce one another, and when they partly offset, depending on borrower liquidity, loan maturity, and bank balance sheets.
